Why We Love Mascots

A mascot gives a team, school, or brand a face you can hug, high-five, or wave at from the upper deck. Mascots lead cheers, visit hospitals, and keep the crowd going when the score isn't.

A Short History

The word comes from the French mascotte, meaning a lucky charm. Early sports mascots were often live animals. Over the twentieth century, costumed performers took over, because a person in a suit can dance, hug fans, and safely walk into a crowd.

What Makes a Good Mascot?

  • Big, readable shapes so fans recognize it from the far seats.
  • Team colors that tie it to the school or club.
  • A friendly face that kids won't find scary up close.
  • A personality with a backstory, a signature move, and a rival.

Life in the Suit

Performers often work in heavy, hot costumes with very limited vision. Most mascots never speak, so every feeling has to come through big gestures. Many have a handler who walks beside them to guide them through crowds and keep them hydrated.

Fun Facts

  • Many colleges hold tryouts for the mascot role, and the performer's name is often kept secret.
  • Some mascot costumes can weigh more than 20 pounds.
  • Mascots usually train to "stay in character" even during breaks in public view.

Hall of Fame

Some well-known mascot types:

  • The Bird: eagles, ducks, and hawks are some of the most common.
  • The Big Cat: tigers, lions, and panthers bring the roar.
  • The Mystery Fuzzball: a creature no one can quite name.
